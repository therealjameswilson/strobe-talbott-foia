SUBJECT: Dealing with Russia 
We're now in the post-Budapest phase of our 
relationship with Russia. Boris Yeltsin's sudden, 
harsh, and very public attack on his friend "Beel" last 
week casts into stark relief a challenge that we've 
recognized for months: how to handle a Russia that 
seems to the world, and certainly to much of our own 
public, to be increasingly quarrelsome, assertive and 
altogether unpartnerlike. 
Meanwhile, the Russians are reciprocating both 
our ahxiety and our annoyance. They're afraid that 
while we continue to pay lip service to support for 
reform and integration, we've actually given up on them 
and are shifting to a new hard line. We're seeing 
diplomatic reporting and intelligence now suggesting 
that the Russians believe that NATO expansicin and the 
$25 billion plus-up of the defense budget are just two 
of many signs that we're abandoning partnership, 
engagement and integration and adopting instead a 
strategy of preemptive containment. In fact, quite a 
few Americans, including some of our own colleagues, 
believe we should do something of that sort. 
That would be a catastrophic mistake. 'Our policy 
would become a self-fulfilling prophecy. By that I do 
not mean to suggest any certainty on my part about

SUBJECT: DEPUTY SECRETARY TALBOTT'S MEETING WITH BELARUSIAN 
OPPOSITION LEADER SANNIKOV 
Classified by Deputy Secretary Strobe Talbott. Reason: 
1.5(d). 
1. (C) Summary. The Deputy Secretary met April 20 with 
former Belarusian Deputy Foreign Minister and current 
opposition leader Andrei Sannikov, international 
coordinator for Charter 97, a broad-based Belarusian 
organization promoting human rights. Sannikov raised 
opposition concerns about renewed agitation for a Slavic 
Union of Russia, Belarus and Yugoslavia and highlighted the 
Belarusian opposition's efforts to hold a presidential 
election on May 16 as a way to focus attention on the 
question of President Lukashenko's legitimacy. Talbott 
said the United States supported voluntary integration and 
association between democratic states, but this was lacking 
in Belarus, and that we would raise our concerns on this 
subject with Russian leaders. On the election initiative, 
Talbott said we would continue coordinating with the 
European Union and Belarus' neighbors to press for 
Belarusian compliance on democracy and human rights. 
Ambassador Daniel Speckhard and S/NIS Principal Deputy Ross 
Wilson also participated in the meeting. DepartmEnt has 
issued a press statement on the meeting. End summary. 
2.--te'+-Deputy,  Secretary Talbott expressed his personal 
sadness over the negative political changes in Belarus 
since his visits in the early 1990s, but said the United 
States would not give up on democracy in Belarus and asked 
for advice on how the U.S. could help. Sannikov conveyed 
what he said was a deep appreciation on the part of the 
entire opposition for the support the United States had 
shown for human rights and democracy in Belarus. 
3.-4G*-Sannikov said the crisis in Yugoslavia had 
exacerbated the opposition's difficulties in Belarus. 
Lukashenko was exploiting the Kosovo crisis to agitate for 
a Slavic union of Belarus, Russia and Yugoslavia, and was 
trying to portray the opposition as*being in league with 
NATO. Lukashenko wanted war, was pursuing a pact with 
Russia and Yugoslavia for this purpose, and would try to 
pull in Ukraine as well. Specifically, Lukashenko had 
offered military assistance to Yugoslavia following a 
meeting with FRY FM Jovanovic in March, and Belarus was 
providing training for Serbian troops and repair facilities 
for tanks and aircraft. In a rhetorical campaign against 
NATO, the United States and new NATO members, Lukashenko 
was telling Belarusians that arms rather than bread were 
needed. There was less skepticism now in Russia and 
Belarus about Lukashenko's prospects to become president of 
Russia.

Sannikov noted U.S. commitments in January 1994 on 
respect for the independence, sovereignty and territorial 
integrity of Belarus, Kazakhstan and Ukraine, following 
their decision to give up nuclear weapons. The independent 
media continued to remind Belarusians of these commitments. 
Talbott noted that he had beenintimately involved in 
formulating U.S. policy in support of the independence of 
all twelve NIS. Sannikov said President Clinton had 
pledged that the United States would help to uphold these 
principles, and that the Belarusian opposition hoped the 
United States would maintain its commitment. Lukashenko's 
•illegal constitutional changes in 1996 had removed the 
legal basis for his regime, which gave the international 
community good reason to take a strong line on any attempts 
by Lukashenko to subvert these principles. The U.S. needed 
to be taking a stronger position against Belarus-Russian 
UNIoN. 
5.-1-CT Talbott noted the U.S. supports, as a general 
principle, voluntary integration and association between 
states, evident in our support for NAFTA, APEC, the EU, and 
other regional associations. S/NIS Principal Deputy Wilson 
added U.S. policy was that integration among the NIS had to 
reflect the voluntary will of the people expressed through 
the democratic process, which currently was lacking in 
Belarus. Sannikov said the deteriorating economic situation 
in Russia and Belarus and the approaching end of President 
Yeltsin's and President Lukashenko's presidential terms had 
lent new urgency to the issue of union. The Russian people 
and the Duma had become more receptiveíto the idea. Talbott 
said Russia understood the folly of assuming the burden of 
the Belarusian economy. Nonetheless, the U.S. would look 
for opportunities to raise Belarus in our bilateral 
dialogue with Russia. 
6.-te)- Turning to the opposition's presidential election 
initiative, Sannikov said the Supreme Soviet's call for an 
election on May 16 was based on-the 1994 constitution, and 
that presidential candidates Mikhail Chigir and Zianon 
Pazniak had obtained the necessary number of signatures and 
been properly registered. Lukashenko had responded with 
intensified repression of everyone organizing the election. 
Central Election Commission chairman Gonchar had been 
imprisoned for ten days and mistreated. Chigir had been 
arrested and his ten-day detention extended to at least 
three months. 
7.  ( ) Sannikov said a meaningful election was not 
possible, but the campaign was raising public awareness 
that Lukashenko's constitutional term in office was ending. 
OSCE representatives in Belarus had noted this awareness. 
Officials around Lukashenko were becoming increasingly 
nervous. Election organizers were encouraged by these 
signs. The declining quality of life for the average 
Belarusian, now little bettert  than in Soviet times, was 
accentuating the opposition's appeal. Sannikov said IMF 
lending to Russia could easily benefit Lukashenko and that 
it was important to ensure this would not happen. Talbott 
said we would raise these concerns in our discussions with 
the IMF.

9.  (C)  On coordinating with other countries, Sannikov said 
Poland was preoccupied with NATO and EU accession but 
needed to take a stronger line with Lukashenko and be less 
concerned with "good neighborly" relations. The 
Lithuanian, Ukrainian and Polish presidents had issued a 
statement critical of developments in Belarus in 1996, but 
there had been no follow-up. Scandinavia was preoccupied 
with the Baltic countries. Germany was giving priority to 
its commercial interests, and Lukashenko was seeking to 
exploit this. U.S.-EU coordination was important, and 
Washington should urge the EU to maintain support for the 
1994 constitution aNd the Supreme Soviet. Sannikov said he 
hoped Ambassador Speckhard would return to Minsk soon.

Talbott said the USG had been coordinating with the EU on

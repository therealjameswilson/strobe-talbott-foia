RELEASE IN FULL 
September 11, 1994 - 
Chris: 
I. had a good, though very brief, visit to Moscow. 
Jim Collins, Nick Burns and Tom Simons met with the 
appropriate Russian officials to plan the Clinton-
Yeltsin summit, while I went off for several intense 
one-on-ones with Mamedov. You're probably getting 
tired of hearing me say it every time I come back from 
one of those encounters, but we are sure lucky to have 
him where he is. He helped defuse several issues that 
could have complicated our lives in coming days, weeks 
and months. 
I did not see Kozyrev, which is probably just as 
well. Andrei was in a towering rage over, of all 
things, Nagorno-Karabakh. There was a CIS prime-
ministerial meeting underway, and Russia is trying to 
hammer out an N-K deal that pretty well cuts CSCE out 
of the play. But your new friend President Aliyev of 
Azerbaijan was balking -- in large measure because 
Madeleine (who had seen him recently in Baku) and the 
Vice President (who had seen him in Cairo) essentially 
encouraged him to hold out for a variant that is more 
to our liking, and more in keeping with our concept of 
international peacekeeping. I'll spare you the 
details. Suffice it to say that Kozyrev was apparently 
furious at us for playing spoilers, but Mamedov helped 
avert a blow-up and we may get a good outcome yet. 
I gave Mamedov a fairly full briefing on what to 
expect in Haiti. I'm confident he won't misuse it in 
any way. Quite the contrary, I think he'll use what I 
gave him to keep the Russians from making too much

I'm quite concerned that we may have re-started 
the NATO-expansion juggernaut without really intending 
to and without sufficient consideration of the 
consequences. 
This concern arises in part becauses of the 
interagency debate over what the Vice President would 
say about NATO in the speech he delivered by satellite 
in Berlin last Friday. There was a push for inclusion 
of a passage that would have been very forward-leaning. 
In one version, the Vice President would have announced 
our intention to begin formal COnsultations within the 
Alliance this fall on a timetable and criteria for 
bringing new members into NATO; he would have announced 
that NATO expansion was on the agenda of the Clinton-
Yeltsin summit. Had it appeared (which it didn't), 
that passage would have encouraged advocates of fast-
track membership for the V-4 countries to believe that, 
at a minimum, a "pledge class" would have been , 
identified by 1996. 
Arousing such expectations would cause trouble all 
around. While there would be applause in Warsaw,-
Budapest, etc., there would almost certainly be a 
severe debate within NATO. That debate will occur no 
matter when the issue of expansion is fully joined, but 
it would be especially divisive and unwelcome now, 
given the severe strains we're undergoing over Bosnia. 
Then, of course, there's the matter of the 
Russians. I know that we as an Administration --
and I personally -- are vulnerable to the charge that 
we're letting Ruspia have a veto of some kind over NATO 
expansion. I am not eager to rekindle that debate. 
But I'm even less eager to see us slip, willy-nilly,

I spent quite a bit of time on this whole subject

arrival here. 
Mamedov believes that we can, if we handle the 
issue properly, ultimately get Russia to accept 
expansion. But the pace and tone and context have to 
be right. Otherwise, the tide could turn seriously 
against Yeltsin internally, with serious external 
ramifications as well. 
Mamedov and others, both in Moscow and among our 
own experts, speculate that if our NATO decision last 
year had gone the other way -- that is, if we'd opted 
for fast-track admission for the V-4 countries -- we 
might have had a very different Russia to contend with 
this year; we might not have gotten the trilateral deal 
with Ukraine, Russian cooperation in the Contact Group, 
and Baltic troop withdrawals (to say nothing•of Russian,
acquiescence on Haiti in the Security Council). 
We've got a lot more of such business to do -- in 
the Trascaucuses, the Balkans and elsewhere.

Hence the attached memo. We've got a two-track 
policy, but we must not be in a rush on either track, 
lest bY pushing too hard on one we derail what's 
happening on the other. Specifically, we must not let 
NATO expansion outpace, and thus undermine, European 
integration. 
You'll see I also make a pitch for not letting PFP 
become a passing fad or OBE. The CEE states have 
joined PFP not for its own sake but because they see it 
as an anteroomIto NATO; Russia has joined mainly 
because it doesn't want there to be any new security 
structures from which it's excluded. If we were to 
declare ourselves in a rush to expand NATO now it would 
vindicate the cyncism of both sides. The Poles would 
pay zero attention to PFP and concentrate entirely on 
getting into NATO, while the Russians would feel 
sucker-punched -- and vitiate the genuine promise of 
PFP itself. 
,It's not going to be easy to keep European 
integration and NATO expansion in sync with each other. 
That's because NATO expansion has a much more vocal, 
politically muscular constituency, and it also sounds, 
relatively easier to achieve: you just open the door a 
crack and admit two or three or four extremely 
enthusiastic appicants to an existing Club. In fact, 
of course, it's actually much harder than that. Poland 
is already asking us, to help pay for their membership 
in PFP (evidence of how far the Poles are from being 
ready for NATO-proper).. We've heard loud exhortations 
from a few Senators who are eager to admit Poland 
(Simon and Lugar, for example) but only the most muted 
caveats from the many more Senators (like Sam'Nunn) who

I. BACKGROUND OF THE PROJECT 
The Fletcher Roundtable was initiated in response to President George Bush's 
call in September 1990 for a "new world order" based on superpower cooperLion, the 
equality of states, and the rule of law. 
The Fletcher School of Law and Diplomacy, in association with the United 
Nations Association of the United States of America (UNA-USA) and The Hitachi 
Foundation, held the first Roundtable meeting on the question of "Defining a New 
World Order." Co-chaired by E11iot L. Richardson and Sir Brian. Urquhart, this meeting 
at the Fletcher School of Law and Diplomacy on May 2 and 3, 1991, brought together 
some thirty experts from ten countries to discuss positive changes in the international 
system that might result from the end of the Cold War and resistance to military 
aggression in the Persian Gulf conflict. For this inaugural session, Alan Henrikson 
prepared a discussion text, Defining a New World Order: Toward a Practical Vision of 
Collective Action for International Peace and Security, and edited Defining a New World 
Order: The Conference Report. Subsequently, Cyrus R. Vance and Olara A. Otunnu 
joined the Roundtable group as additional co-chairmen. 
The second convening of the Roundtable, which was organized by the Fletcher 
School, the UNA-USA, and The Hitachi Foundation, took place on January 29-31, 1992, 
at Captiva Island, Florida. This meeting focused specifically on the problem of 
"Collective Security in an Evolving World Order." A report in the form of an essay was 
prepared by Jeffrey Laurenti, Directions and Dilemmas in Collective Security: Reflections 
from a Global Roundtable. Subsequently, an analysis of the consideration of the subject, 
The Evolving World Order.• The State of Deliberations, was written by Nigel Gould-Davies 
with further support from The Hitachi Foundation. 
During the year 1991-92 the Fletcher Roundtable on a New World Order 
organized a number of related 'events at the Fletcher School. These included public 
lectures by Maynard W. Glitman, Kishore Mahbubani, Richard W. Murphy, and 
Gebhardt von MOItke. Mahbubani, author of "The West and the Rest," The National 
Interest, no. 28 (Summer 1992), also was a participant in the January 1992 Captiva 
conference.

M. CURRENT WORK AND FUTURE PLANS 
The Fletcher Roundtable on a New World Order will continue to address the 
major themes of global stability, efficiency, and equity with a view toward anticipating 
and intelligently managing international problems by means of diplomacy. 
The Fletcher Roundtable will carry on the work of the Fletcher School of Law 
and Diplomacy in the important fields of international organization and multilateral 
negotiation with particular attention to peace and security issues, examined in historical 
context. This work, which started with the inauguration of the Fletcher School in 1933, 
was renewed with the 1983-84 Negotiating World Order Project, celebrating the School's 
50th Anniversary with a series of lectures by leading practitioners of international affairs. 
This initiative was a response also to the obvious need at that time (the "second Cold 
War") for greater attention to diplomacy on the part of the two nuclear superpowers, 
globally and in critical regions. Alan K. Henrikson, ed., Negotiating World Order: The 
Artisanship and Architecture of Global Diplomacy (Scholarly Resources Inc., 1986), 
embodied these statements and discussions, and-related-them-to-current scholarship and 
to continuing international developments.

In the field of curriculum development, the Fletcher Roundtable on a New World 
Order is designed to augment, with practical lessons of direct experience gained from 
many Roundtable conference participants, the formal course offerings of the Fletcher 
School of Law and Diplomacy. The principal course associated with the Roundtable is 
the Seminar on the Negotiation of World Order (Diplomacy 202). This seminar, to be 
offered again in the Spring term of 1994, examines the "architecture," or 
international-organizational frameworks, and the "artisanship," or the skilled diplomatic 
craftsmanship, by which global order-building is achieved. In terms of substantive policy, 
the seminar focuses on the three central international questions of controlling weapons 
proliferation, liberalizing world trade, and protecting the globe's environment and 
managing its resources. It encourages student-participants to develop individual projects 
for academic study and, possibly, for actual future international diplomatic action. 
Diplomacy 202 is part of the "United States," "International Negotiation and_ _ 
Conflict Resolution," and "International Organization" fields as outlined in the current 
catalogue of the Fletcher School of Law and Diplomacy.

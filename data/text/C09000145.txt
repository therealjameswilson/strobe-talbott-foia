September 12, 1994 
. MEMORANDUM TO THE SECRETARY 
FROM: STROBE TALBOTT 
SUBJECT: THE FUTURE OF EUROPEAN SECURITY 
This Administration is committed to both European 
integration and NATO expansion. In January, President Clinton 
established as part of his vision for the future that the end of 
the Cold War gives the international community its first 
opportunity since the birth of the nation state to build a Europe 
that is no longer divided into competing alliances and blocs. He 
has also said that NATO has its own role to play in the process 
of European integration, and that it is,a question of wham, not 
whether, NATO will permit the entry of new members, presumably 
'from among the northern CEE states. Vice President Gore 
reaffirmed these twin pillars of our Europe policy in his 
televised speech to the Berlin conference last Friday. 
Our Administration, working with its West European allies, 
has undertaken a number of -initiatives that demonstrate we are 
serious about opening doors to the East -- and that letting some 
before others into NATO won't necessarily mean erecting new 
barriers. Our leadership in the G-7 to support post-Communist 
reform and our Economies in Transition initiative are just two 
examples. We have also, in NACC and PFP, inaugurated the first 
genuinely inclusive post-Cold War security arrangements. PFP is 
especially relevant, of course, because it is a way of advancing 
both goals -- European integration and NATO expansion; it is

An expanded NATO in an integrated Europe is not a 
contradiction. But keeping it from becoming one requires 
conceptual sophistication, deft statesmanship, consistency, 
patience -- and disciplined interagency consideration of tactics 
and strategy alike. 
A year ago we adopted an approach that took full account of 
the fluidity and complexity of the situation in Europe. The key 
elements of that approach were the following: 
-- NATO is the most successful military alliance in history. 
It preserved peace in Europe for nearly half a century by 
deterring the Soviet threat. Even though the Warsaw'Pact 
has been disbanded and the USSR dissolved, NATO should be 
retained, and kept at the ready, in case a neW threat should 
arise to the East. 
-- At the same time, NATO should be made relevant to the 
task of managing new, post-Cold War conflicts within Europe. 
We are trying, albeit with great difficulty, to do 
precisely that in the former Yugoslavia, where NATO has been 
used to enforce the will of the United Nations Security 
Council. 
-- Finally, NATO should(also be a mechanism for int'egrating 
into the West the emerging democracies of the East. 
This last objective is crucial -- and also complicated. 
NATO can serve the cause of integration in two ways: 1) by 
encouraging, through NACC and PFP, "habits of cooperation" and 
fostering a sense of common security across the old Iron Curtain; 
and 2) by accepting new members from the ranks of the former 
Warsaw Pact. 
There is a potential tension between these two aspects of 
NATO's evolution. NACC/PFP is an ,inclusive institution (come 
one,come all), which is already up and running; NATO expansion, 
by contrast, is necessarily going to be an exclusive process (not

The issue of NATO's future will figure prominently at 
several upcoming events: the U.S.-Russian summit at the end of 
the month (President Yeltsin wants to hear President Clinton's 
thinking on the subject); the CSCE summit in December (what is 
the relationship between NATO and CSCE?); and the winter NATO 
ministerial (will criteria and timetable be on the agenda?). I 
would urge that our preparation for those events be guided by 
three points: 
-- 1) Let's be careful not to shift energy away from PFP, or 
give others an excuse to do so. This signature initiative of the 
Clinton Administration is still very much in its infancy. It 
needs both time and high-level attention. While it is 
appropriate and necessary to broaden and deepen our discussion of 
how NATO will expand in the future, we must not let our handling 
of that issue undercut our efforts on behalf of PFP as a program 
in the here and now. 
If we appear to have lost interest and confidence in the 
'campaign on behalf of PFP and thrown ourselves instead into the 
NATO-expansion debate, it will confirm suspicions that PFP was 
just a diversionary tactic, a gimmick or a consolation prize of 
some kind. Those who see NATO membership as the be-all-and-end-
all of their security will step up their demands for fast-track 
admission, and those who worry that NATO expansion will be 
detrimental to their interests will step up their blocking 
tactics.. Rational debate and a sensible process will suffer. 
Therefore so will our overarching cause of an undivided, 
integrated Europe. 
2) Let's develop a more nuanced rationale for NATO 
expansion, one that supports the vision of integration rather 
than seeming like an insurance policy against the failure of 
integration. A year ago, we said that the expansion of NATO 
would depend in part on the "security environment" in Europe. By

that we meant -- and were clearly understood to mean -- that if 
Russia "went bad," we'd hasten the entry of the CEE states to 
protect them from newly aggressive Russian behavior. That 
remains a valid theme in our doctrine and in our contingency 
planning ("prepare for the worst while trying to bring about the 
best"). But it must not stand alone as a reason to expand NATO. 
For one thing, it provides an artificial and unwelcome incentive 
for aspirants to and advocates of an expanded NATO to proclaim 
and even gloat over the failure of reform in Russia. For 
another, it suggests that NATO expansion will, when it occurs, by 
definition be punishment, or "neo-containment," of the bad Bear. 
We need to make,the case for NATO to expand in a way that 
supports RusSian reform even if the Alliance does not admit a 
reformist Russia to full meinbership in the first (or even the 
second or third) wave of new members. 
During my brief visit to Moscow Friday and Saturday, I found 
officials of the Yeltsin government very alert to signals from 
Washington on this subject. They have moved some distance from 
where they were in January, when Yeltsin insisted publicly that 
there could be no new members from the East until Russia gets in 
-- a demand that President Clinton firmly refuted. I think some 
Russians are beginning to adjust to the near-inevitability of 
expansion on a country-by-country basis. Moreover, they show 
signs of beginning to entertain discussion of the possibility 
that NATO expansion might be managed in a way that will actually 
serve a reformist Russia's security and political interests. 
(The Vice President laid the groundwork for that case in his 
Berlin speech.) 
3) We must not think about NATO in a vacuum; we've got to 
look at it in the context of the other institutions and groupings 
that are to some extent cooperating, and to some extent vying, 
with each other for influence over the nature and shape of the 
new Europe. 
The NATO-expansion discussion to date has been too narrowly 
focUsed. We must address more broadly and systematically the 
interrelated functions of NATO, NACC, PFP, CSCE, EU/WEU, the UN 
and, yes, the CIS in the emergence of tomorrow's Europe.

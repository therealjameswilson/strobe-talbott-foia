This a follow-up to our letter to you of March 25, 1994 on Haiti to which you 
responded on May 25, 1994. In that letter the Woman's National Democratic Club 
urged you to take stronger action against the repressive military regime in that country. 
Now that the US armed forces are in Haiti and in a position to enforce General Raoul 
Cédras's promise to relinquish power on or before October 15, we hope that it will be 
U.S. policy not only to have Cédras and his military colleagues step down but to leave 
the country. Their continued presence in Haiti will pose a grave threat to the safety of 
President Jean-Bertrand Aristide and to the stability of his fragile government. Their 
presence will also invite vengeance from Aristide supporters and thus contribute to civil 
unrest. 
Indeed, Mr. President, we wish to express our deep concern about U.S. plans to 
maintain a Haitian arrny, even a retrained one.. Given the record of the Haitian armed 
forces, we do not have any confidence that future training will be any more successful 
than that provided by the United States in the past. We urge you to use this 
opportunity to reduce and disarm the Haitian armed forces and, eventually, to abolish 
them once the Haitian constitution can be amended to eliminate any mention of the 
military. The history of the military throughout Latin America, not just in Haiti, 
demonstrates the seriousness 0f the threat posed by these armed forces to 
democratically elected civilian governments. If an army is permitted to remain in Haiti, 
it is probable that any advances toward democratic rule will be nullified. The only 
hope for democracy in Haiti lies in demilitarization -- the abolition of the regular armed 
forces, the paramilitary groups, and the restructuring of the current police force into a 
small, well-trained civilian force capable of maintaining law and order. We believe that 
is what President Aristide favors. 
After demilitarization and provision of food and medicine, the most pressing need in 
Haiti is for education. Worid Bank studies indicate that unless more than 50 percent of 
a country's population is literate there can be no hope for sustainable development. 
With 80 percent illiteracy in Haiti no amount of aid can be expected to alleviate that 
country's dire poverty until the illiteracy problem is tackled on a massive scale. We also

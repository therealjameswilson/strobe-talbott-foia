1. 4.E} SUMMARY: German MoD State Secretary Stuetzle 
emphasized to DepSec Talbott February 4 the need to 
conduct a "sunshine summit" in Washington in April, 
i.e., one focused on a clear Strategic Concept that 
common citizens can comprehend and on NATO's role in 
expanding the Transatlantic region of prosperity and 
security in the next century. Operationally, Stuetzle 
said, this meant that summit planners should stop what 
he saw as the proliferation of annexes, "vision 
statements" and initiatives. On the individual issues, 
Stuetzle said the following: 
ESDI: It was unquestioned that the goal was for 
Europeans to do something substantial in security and 
defense within the Alliance, not independently. The 
positive element of St. Malo was Britain's clear turn 
toward constructive participation in this facet of 
European integration. 
Mandates for NATO deployments: A clear legal basis was 
required for enforcement or "enabling" of peace 
agreements, but no one wanted to "put NATO decision-
making under UN authority." Unanimity within the 
Alliance was more important than the nature of a 
mandate from without. 
"Open Door" and "Naming Names": Stuetzle favored summit 
language that named no new invitees or spurred 
hopes that could not be fulfilled. Told by the DepSec 
that it seemed (after Talbott's Paris consultations the 
day before) that naming all nine aspirants might be 
unavoidable, Stuetzle replied that Germany would do 
whatever was possible to "not block consensus." 
WMD: The summit should make it clear to the public that 
we see a danger and that we have means to contain this 
danger through political deterrence and arms control. 
However, the Strategic Concept should not be turned 
into a "major WMD document" setting out extensive 
detail. 
CFE: The U.S. and Germany agree on maintaining a firm 
line with Moscow and on seeking reductions in national 
ceilings from all states in the Center, including but 
not limited to the three invitees.

The "Sunshine Summit" 
3. +€4 German MoD State Secretary Walter Stuetzle told 
DepSec Talbott February 4 in Bonn it was of fundamental 
importance that the German public understand that it 
was NATO and the U.S. in NATO that "took care" of the 
successful 50-year development of democracy and 
prosperity here. Consequently, the Washington Summit 
should be a "sunshine summit" emphasizing the 
Alliance's future role and continuing necessity in 
protecting and expanding the transatlantic region of 
prosperity. Attention should not be diverted from the 
Strategic Concept, which should be a fundamental 
political document written in language our publics, but 
also audiences in Russia, Ukraine, and China, will 
understand. Stuetzle asked that we narrow down the 
number of documents, annexes, and vision statements 
being developed, in what he found an overly 
bureaucratic approach to the summit. 
4. +ei- DepSec Talbott agreed that Washington wanted a 
summit which emphasized unity of purpose among allies 
and one which did not duplicate the last-minute , 
suspense and teeth-gnashing of Madrid. He took , 
Stuetzle's point about not making a Christmas tree 
of the Strategic Concept and other summit documents, 
but argued that our other initiatives were much more 
than "decorative" (details follow).

Open Door/Naming Names 
6. -f.e4 Stuetzle said he was "amazed" by the debate 
over these terms. He read aloud from what he 
understood to be U.S.-proposed, Madrid-plus language in 
which no aspirant states were named, and noted that he 
found the approach fully acceptable. He favored summit 
language that named no new invitees or spurred hopes 
that could not be fulfilled. Talbott replied that 
after the consultations he had had the day before in 
Paris, he did not think "naming no names" would be 
possible. Rather, we probably would have to name nine 
aspirant countries, although the U.S. saw the 
disadvantages of doing so. France, Spain, Italy 
and Turkey each wanted to secure a "moment in the sun" 
for one or another of the Central or South-Central 
Europeans, and then the Balts could not be left out. 
Stuetzle found U.S. preparedness to name names 
"interesting news" but added: "We'll do whatever is 
possible not to block consensus." Talbott thanked him 
for this constructive posture.

8. -(t9--DepSec identified CFE as one of two potential 
clouds over the "sunshine summit." He had the 
impression Germany and the U.S. were doing well 
together, but the "cloud" lay in managing relations 
with Russia. Moscow anxiously sought agreement on the 
key elements of an adapted treaty before the Washington 
Summit. Stuetzle saw no problem between Bonn and 
Washington on how to approach Russia. Talbott noted 
the U.S. believed reductions in national ceilings of 
states in the center was key to a solution. Most of 
the states in question were committed to this approach, 
but we still needed to secure a firm commitment from 
Poland. Stuetzle agreed, noting that DefMin Scharping 
already planned to endorse this strongly to his Polish 
counterpart. The Central Europeans should be 
encouraged to relate CFE to their integration into the 
Alliance and not in terms of "preparing for war with 
Russia."

10. -tet - In a similar vein, Stuetzle questioned the 
proposed membership action plan (MAP) . Having heads of 
government issue an "action plan" was apt to generate 
impressions and hopes that would be time-consuming and 
expensive, financially and politically, to fulfill. 
If there must be an action plan, could it not be agreed 
by the Foreign Ministers as a separate action and not 
be part of a summit declaration or the Strategic 
Concept, he asked. Talbott replied that he understood 
these concerns.  We viewed the MAP as a way to make 
enlargement real and practical, to keep the process 
credible and disciplined and keep it from becoming a 
"beauty contest." The action plan would require 
concrete progress by aspirant states. 
Kosovo 
11. -E-C-4- At the outset of the meeting, Talbott said if 
things went truly badly in Kosovo, it would completely 
overshadow even the best prepared summit. He asked for 
Stuetzle's views on the potential deployment of ground 
troops. Stuetzle said the dramatic ACTORD vote in the 
waning days of the last Bundestag had been justified by 
the hope of preventing a humanitarian disaster. As 
long as the threat of a humanitarian disaster loomed in 
Kosovo, Germany's support for potential military action 
would not change. The Alliance must not diverge, 
Stuetzle continued, from a "clear, secure, NATO line of 
command and control." A deployment, be it as an 
"extraction" or "protection" force, would be a very 
long (well beyond two-year) op6ration, and could likely 
not be confined to Kosovo indefinitely.

12. 4et-Talbott dismissed the "bugbear" that the U.S. 
intended to "take NATO global" and said he would 
address the point extensively in his public address 
later that day. Stuetzle said the question of giving 
NATO a global role or not was itself misplaced. NATO 
had for a long time been playing a global role of 
securing peace and prosperity over a significant 
portion of the globe and its population. In an EU of 
potentially 25 countries, one-seventh of all UN member 
states and over half a billion people would be 
represented. It was an important piece of global 
stability to see to it that NATO held North America and 
an expanding EU together in stability. 
13. -+ei-Comment: On the mandate issue, Germany's 
preference is to have a UNSC mandate as the legal basis 
for deploying Bundeswehr troops out of area. However, 
when this is impossible to achieve and when there is 
the danger of an humanitarian crisis in Europe, the 
German government, whether under Kohl or Schroeder, has 
been confident it could muster the required Bundestag 
majority from conservative parties and from individual 
Social Democrats and Greens who believe Germany has a 
moral responsibility, because of its history, to act to 
prevent ethnic massacres in Europe. Regarding, WMD, 
the German MOD has resisted from the outset any 
requirement for substantial new resources - hence 
Stuetzle's focus on containing WMD through political 
deterrence and arms control.

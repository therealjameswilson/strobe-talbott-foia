I am writing in response to your request yesterday evening at dinner 
for specific suggestions as well as general observations regarding 
possible approaches for assisting Russia and the other states of the 
former Soviet Union. At the risk of presumption, I would like'to 
sumMarize certain Congressional apprehensions and suggest a policy 
approach somewhat at variance with the tenor of the conversation 
last night. 
It strikes me that Members acknowledged the necessity for singular 
U.S. leadership in developing an effective international strategy to 
support the reform process in the former Soviet Union, but expressed 
uneasiness at the implications of singular U.S. financial 
responsibility for such support. Once the Congress and the nation 
assume an American commitment to action, burden sharing becomes the 
issue. 
In this regard, it cannot be overstated that the United States plays 
a leading role in international institutions which by their very 
nature compel burden sharing: the International Monetary Fund 
(IMF), the World Bank, and the European Bank for Reconstruction and 
Development (EBRD). While there is understandable skepticism about 
primary reliance on international financial institutions (IFIs) to 
administer creative new policies, such as housing programs or a 
ruble stabilization fund, the most constructive way to gain 
international support for any reform program we may wish to lead is 
through these institutions. There is no reason why, for example, a 
new facility cannot be created within any or all of these 
,institutions for the specific purpose of supporting particular or 
more general initiatives in the former Soviet Union. Further, it is 
clear that many other governments are much more willing to support 
aid through these institutions than they are through a purely 
American or western initiative.

Tom Lantos constructively suggested that you simply pick up the 
phone and call King Fahad, the Emir of Kuwait, the Sultan of Brunei, 
and President Lee in Taiwan and ask for multi-billion dollar 
commitments to a U.S. initiative. My sense is that it is more 
difficult for them to provide money to the United States 
at our request to help Russia than to contribute through the 
international financial institutions, which in the case of some 
countries might even be the Asian Development Bank (ADB). In fact, 
one might suggest that the ADB be employed to help effect the 
transition from centrally-planned to market economies in the Russian 
Far East or Central Asia. 
The Vice President indicated frustration with the conditionality 
that is a prerequisite to international financial institution 
lending, and David Obey objected to the IMF's "cookie cutter" 
approach. But the fact is that all governments have like complaints 
about the IFIs and these complaints should be taken with a grain of 
salt. Far from a weakness, one of the strengths of these 
institutions is that they are by design intended to press for 
fundamental reforms -- price liberalization, macroeconomic 
stabilization, and the restructuring-of financial institutions) and 
markets -- and therefore carry the burden of blame for what may be 
seen as outside interference. It is a plus -- not a minus -- that 
Russians complain about the World Bank and IMF rather than American, 
French, or German conditionality. 
In this regard, I would like to reiterate that one of the advantages 
of the IFIs is that the U.S. contribution, depending on the 
institution, varies from 10 to 20 percent, which implies that the 
rest of the world accepts 80 to 90 percent of the obligations. In 
addition to leveraging dollars, these institutions allow the United 
States to leverage principles. Few governments are prone to bow to 
pressure for market-oriented reform coming from a single country. 
Many, however, will institute politically difficult reforms as a 
prerequisite for IMF and World Bank support and the seal of approval 
for commercial bank lending such support implies.

At issue is American leadership and how we can most effectively 
leverage the contributions we make. If we were to put new emphasis 
on the IFIs or establish a new facility within one or all of them 
for specific purposes, there is a significant possibility that Japan 
-- which is reluctant to provide significant bilateral aid because 
of the Northern Territories dispute -- as well as Lantos's big four 
-- Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Brunei, and Taiwan -- would be willing to 
play an important role in American led efforts. 
In ,411 development efforts there is a resentment factor, in both the 
aid giving and the recipient country. It was mentioned that a top 
priority the Administration has in mind is assistance for Russian 
housing. But however compelling the need, especially as it may 
relate to providing incentives for roop withdrawals from the 
Baltics, the Administration must come to grips with the question of 
whether a program should be initiated as a U.S. bilateral effort or 
come under the aegis of an international financial institution. As 
a U.S. program there may be more resentment in America than anyone 
can now gauge and, if viewed in Moscow as a quasi-strategic 
initiative, it could conceivably be counterproductive. If, on the 
other hand, a housing program were part of a multi-lateral 
initiative, reactions might be more positive in both our countries. 
Just as the United States used a U.N. mandate in the Gulf War to 
galvanize and channel international support, we ought not to shy 
away from utilizing another FDR legacy, the Bretton Woods 
institutions. 
There is also no reason the United States should not take the lead 
in another aspect of burden sharing, debt restructuring. By 
definition, debt restructuring puts 90 percent of the burden on 
Europe and Japan because they hold the lion's share of the debt. 
Obviously, you as President run a high risk of alienating the 
Germans and French in particular if you forcefully push debt 
reduction, but that does not mean America should not be at the 
cutting edge of progressivity on the issue. Perhaps my suggestion 
that all debt assumed under the communist system (about $68 billion) 
be written off is extreme, but the United States has a moral basis 
for taking the debt reduction lead in two respects: (a) the 
historical legacy of the Marshall Plan in Europe, in which we 
restructured a great deal of debt and in response to which Europe 
should be prepared to restructure their debt with Russia; and (b) 
the Brady Plan model for Latin America where our banks and 
government are taking the largest share of the loss.

In the long term, it should be stressed that structural ' 
transformation in the former Soviet Union is unlikely to take place 
without a transformation in attitudes, and this can best be 
accomplished by more frequent contact with the West, especially 
America. The American public is both extraordinarily pragmatic and 
idealistic about the potentialities of exchanges and as you may 
know, Senator Bradley and I coauthored an amendment to last year's 
Freedom Exchange Act that provides about $50 million for exchange 
programs. Given the long-term benefits these programs would provide 
to the United States, there is no reason why that amount should not 
be doubled or tripled.

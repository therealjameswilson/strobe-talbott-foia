2. summary. nizhegorod governor nemtsov, who was in the 
united states primarily to attend the arden house 
conference at harvard university and to meet with bankers 
in new york city, began his april 4 meeting with the 
deputy secretary by-criticizing the usg's sharp reduction 
in assistance to russia. he argued that russians need to 
see concrete results from western aid programs and praised 
the officer housing project as visibly useful assistance. 
nemtsov assessed yeltsin's chances of re-election as 
twenty percent. he cited the communist party's 
organizational superiority, particularly its ability to 
mobilize at the grass roots level, as its greatest asset. 
nemtsov has a low,estimation of zyuganov's intellectual 
capacity and believes he does not know what he will do --
particularly in the economic sphere -- if he actually 
finds himself in power. he offered suggestions on how the 
u.s. should approach the runup to the elections. general 
sheehan (cinc/ant), who will trave-1 to russia shortly, 
joined the deputy secretary at the conclusion of the 
meeting. nemtsov advised him of the intensity of 
anti-NATO sentiment in russia and suggested that he 
postpone his trip until after the presidential elections. 
end summary. 
3. nemtsov began the meeting by attacking the usg's 
"short-sightedness" in reducing assistance to russia to 
"almost zero.1, he argued that even the.previous higher 
level of assistance represented a negligible portion of 
the overall u.s. budget whereas the enhancement of u.s. 
influence in russia as a result of properly targeted 
assistance programs was inestimable. he agreed that the 
russian people had become disenchanted with -- and even 
hostile to -- assistance programs because,too many were 
devoted to producing paper studies, by americans for 
americans, that were never realized. the russian public 
needs to see concrete results from aid in order to view 
cooperation with the west as beneficial. nemtsov praised 
the officer housing project in particular as a tangible 
demonstration of u.s. interest in helping ease russia's 
economic and political transition. it had

4. the deputy secretary asked nemtsov for his opinion of 
exchange programs. nemtsov felt they were useful but did 
not reach a great enough range of people. he pointed out 
that nizhegorod, with 3.7 million residents, had only had 
15 young people involved in exchanges, and "they were not 
°key' people." he stressed his preference for exchanges 
focused on sectoral specialties, such as agriculture, 
which could impart more concrete skills. he also 
expressed a desire to cooperate more actively with 
nizhniy-novgorod's sister city philadelphia. 
5. in response to the deputy secretary's questions about 
the political environment prior to the presidential 
elections, nemtsov said that certain weekly polls, which 
he considers to be highly accurate, estimate zyuganov's 
strength at 45 percent in rural areas, 15 percent in urban 
areas, and 26 percent overall. Yeltsin lags at twenty 
percent in the rural areas, 11 percent in the urban areas, 
and 14-15 percent overall. The president has gained in 
the pdlls, since a few months ago when he was hovering 
around 6 percent in the oblast. but zyuganov in recent 
months has also risen above his initial 18,percent rating, 
putting to rest the notion that zyuganov is doomed to hold 
steady at approximately 25 percent of the vote. 
6. nemtsov suggested several reasons for the communists' 
success. the cprf is putting its formidable organization, 
with approximately 700,000 volunteers nationwide, to 
effective use in campaigning. nemtsov observed that 
air time leads to the erroneous impression that 
the communists are not a political force in russia, since 
they seldom appear on television. the yeltsin people 
overvalue television as a campaign medium, and yeltsin 
supporters in the major networks have intentionally 
limited coverage of the communists. the communists, on 
the other hand, feel the battle will be waged at the grass 
roots level and that television is relatively immaterial 
in mobilizing the electorate. 
7. nemtsov quoted stalin that "it doesn't matter who, 
votes but who counts." nemtsov claimed that the 
communists, with their highly developed poll monitoring 
teams, are likelier to commit fraud in the tabulation and 
posting of ballots than are yeltsin's people.

8. nemtsov expressed concern that a yavlinskiy candidacy 
could siphon critical votes from yeltsin and prevent 
the president from making it to the second round. he 
described relations between yeltsin and yavlinskiy as 
terrible -- they were like "two red-eyed bulls facing off 
in a meadow." he asked the usg to use its good offices 
to prevail upon yeltsin t& mend fences with yavlinskiy and 
secure his promise not to run. nemtsov claims he has 
twice advised yeltsin to patch things up with yavlinskiy 
but has been ignored due to korzhakov's influence. 
9. nemtsov claims that his personal relations with 
yeltsin are still quite good, although he is aware that 
many in the president's circle were angered by his 
Chechnya petition initiative and have tried to turn the 
president against him. nemtsov also indicated that 
yeltsin had incorporated many suggestions previously made 
by nemtsov into his recently announced peace plan. 
10. nemtsov assesses that zyuganov's chances of winning 
as very good, approximately eighty percent. He termed the 
possibility of a communist victory a "tragedy" for the 
country, particularly because zyuganov understands nothing 
about economics. nemtsov has been demonized by the 
Communists as an enemy of the people simply because he has 
undertaken reforms. the communists have succeeded in 
portraying the economic transformation as a western plot 
directed against the well-being of the russian people, and 
it is very difficult for yeltsin to defend himself against 
such accusations. 
11. nemtsov described his efforts to invent a new 
terminology to describe reform concepts because the word 
reform now has such pejorative connotations for the 
russian people. he prefers to characterize his programs 
as "reconstruction" or "renovation." he has found that 
residents of nizhegorod are much more receptive to these 
denotations of reform. 
12. nemtsov thinks zyuganov does not know what he would 
do if he finds himself in power. he has no intellectual 
basis, beyond some 'vague notions about russia's 
restoration as a great power, for his ideological 
program. in general nemtsov has a low opinion of 
zyuganoV''s intellectual capacity.

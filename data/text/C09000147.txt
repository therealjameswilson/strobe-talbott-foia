I've tried, during my time off, to step back a bit 
and look at the whole picture. What I see is an 
Administration that is grappling conscientiously with 
all the right questions and already, less than halfway 
through what we hope will be the first of two terms, 
coming up with the right answers. Yet I also see what 
the world'sees, which is an Administration that appears 
to be stumbling,about. 
One of the most basic requirements that the 
American people look for in a President is the ability 
to convey a sense of having clearly in focus a fairly 
simple set of principles for dealing with a complicated 
world. Granted, that was a lot easier in the Cold War. 
Every President from Harry Truman to George Bush, 
halfway through his term, could get up in the morning, 
look himself in the mirror, and ask, "What's my 
principal obligation as Commander in Chief today?" 
and the answer was indeed-simple: to hold the line 
against the Soviets and their minions, while keeping 
the specter of nuclear destruction at bay. If, at the 
end of the day, there had been no setbacks in those two 
respects, he could go to bed that night knowing that he 
had earned his paycheck as Leader of the Free World --
and knowing that a working majority of his constituents 
would agree. 
One reason Bush failed at re-election was that by 
the time he faced the voters in 1992, the foreign-
policy compOnent of the presidential job description 
had changed in some fundamental way that he could not 
adequately describe or adjust to. He knew that the 
collapse of the Soviet Union required thinking, and 
talking, about U.S. foreign-policy interests in a new 
way. But he couldn't quite figure out how.

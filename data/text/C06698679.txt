2. (21 SUMMARY:  Deputy Secretary Talbot told Polish and 
Czech Deputy Foreign Ministers in Washington October 3 for 
bilateral security talks that NATO enlargement will proceed 
on schedule and will not be linked to negotiation of a 
NATO-Russian Charter, NATO adaptation, or domestic politics 
in Russia. The Deputy Secretary said he expects Russia to 
continue efforts to slow or derail enlargement, and 
stressed U.S. determination to proceed. He cautioned that 
as NATO identifies the first candidates for membership, we 
must make clear that the door remains open for others. The 
Deputy Secretary noted that in his estimation even the 
prospect of NATO membership has already had a salutary 
effect on the developing democracies of the region. Polish 
DFM Wyzner and Czech DEM Vondra each expressed concerns 
about Russia's attempts and potential to slow or disrupt 
the process, but both were extremely pleased with the 
Deputy Secretary's candid explication of U.S. policy and 
reassurances on the current status of NATO expansion. END 
SUMMARY. 
3. ...(erst The Deputy Secretary met October 3 with Czech 
Deputy Foreign Minister Alexandr Vondra, Polish First 
Deputy Foreign Minister Eugeniusz Wyzner, and Czech and 
Polish delegations to bilateral arms control and security 
talks with each country hosted by Under Secretary Lynn 
Davis. The Czech and Polish delegations included 
representatives from the MFA and Ministry of Defense. From 
the U.S. side also attending were U/S Davis, EUR/NCE 
Director Rickert, D and T staff (notetaker) 
4. hei The Deputy Secretary said that NATO will enlarge on 
schedule. He added that President Clinton wants to see the 
process bear fruit if he is reelected. The Deputy 
Secretary continued that the West under the leadership of 
the U.S. needs a sensible plan for involving Russia in the 
process of NATO enlargement. He pointed to the Secretary's 
proposal of a NATO-Russia Charter, which we have discussed 
with the Russians, including a long conversation between 
the Secretary and Foreign Minister Primakov at the UNGA. 
The Deputy Secretary said this Charter would not be a 
legally binding document, but would affirm the post-Cold 
War mission and opportunities of the Alliance. Russia

5. „LC.). The Deputy Secretary said that Russia is on the 
horns of a dilemma of its own making -- it does not want 
NATO enlarged, but it also wants a decent, stable 
relationship with NATO, even if enlarged. The Russians 
fear acceptance of a Charter might be perceived as 
endorsing NATO expansion. Russia could be pursuing a two-
track policy, doing all they can to .slow NATO expansion but 
having an insurance policy in the form of the Charter. The 
Deputy"Secretary asserted that our position is simple -- we 
have only one track -- to work out a modus vivendi. 
6. j,21 The Deputy Secretary pointed out at least three 
unknown factors for the Russians in this process. First, 
U.S. domestic politics -- Yeltsin and Primakov may still 
have doubts about President Clinton's resolve. They are 
wrong, but they won't believe this until after November 5, 
when they will hear a re-elected President Clinton clearly 
reiterate his position. Second, Russian domestic politics, 
where there is a cloud over the leadership. This is a 
factor whose effects are hard to assess, except to admit it 
is there. Third and most sensitive, the Russians continue 
to hope against hope they can split the Alliance. The U.S. 
needs to do more with its key Allies. In this respect, 
President Chirac's recent visit to Warsaw was helpful. 
7. per The Deputy Secretary explained the U.S. has a simple 
schema for modernizing European security institutions, 
which proceeds on four parallel, independent tracks: NATO 
internal adaptation; NATO enlargement; developing the NATO-
Russian relationship; and CFE and other arms control. Each 
track has important benchmark dates. For example, the 
December 1996 ministerial and first half of 1997 NATO 
Summit are benchmarks on the expansion track. We might 
look for progress on a NATO-Russian Charter by the Lisbon 
OSCE Summit. Under Secretary Davis said the U.S. hopes the 
Lisbon OSCE Summit would be an important benchmark in the 
process of adaptation of the OFE Treaty. U/S Davis noted 
that NATO would table a paper next week in Vienna on the 
scope and parameters of CFE adaptation which was based on 
an earlier paper form the four "Visegrad" states. We hoped 
these states would sign on as co-sponsors to the NATO 
paper. Progress on the arms control track was underway, 
and Russia would see it moving.

9. DFM Vondra asked which countries might be named at 
the NATO Summit. The Deputy Secretary replied that in his 
view every democratizing, non-communist, reforming state in 
Europe should be eligible. This extends to Russia, 
although some West Europeans would rule out Russia (this is 
a controversial subject) . However, he emphasized, the door 
to NATO must remain open. NATO entry should be on a case-
by-case basis. Those not in the first tranche of new 
members who think they should be must not be left out. DFM 
Vondra also inquired which state the Deputy Secretary 
considered most important strategically, leaving out 
democracy -- Slovenia or Slovakia. The Deputy Secretary 
rejoined "but I won't leave out democracy," and alluded to 
concerns the U.S. has about Slovakia. Overall, the Deputy 
Secretary asserted, the very prospect of being NATO members 
(and thus meeting the broad set of criteria for membership) 
has had a salutary effect on the region, and cited the 
recent Hungarian-Romanian Treaty as an example of progress 
on a formerly intractable problem. 
10. X Polish Ambassador Kozminski inquired whether the 
U.S. is worried about other states creating linkage between 
NATO adaptation and enlargement. The Deputy Secretary 
replied there is some danger of that, but we are in close 
contact and consultation with one unnamed Ally most 
interested in this. We think the differences on this are 
tactical, and we are confident we will be able to resolve 
them. DFM Vondra asked whether the clouds over the 
Russian leadership might slow the process of enlargement. 
The Deputy Secretary replied that our position is that they 
should not, but if the situation in Moscow grows murky, 
some might urge a pause. We think this would be a big 
mistake. If the Russians think that their internal crises

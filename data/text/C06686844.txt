Thank you for your letter of September 1, enclosing a copy 
of your letter to President Clinton on the subject of barter 
and countertrade as a means to increase trade with the former 
republics of the Soviet Union. 
I found your letter interesting and have given the question 
of barter serious consideration. We share your goal of 
increasing trade and investment in Russia and the other New 
Independent States (NIS). Russia's lack of hard currency but 
wealth of natural resources poses both challenges and 
opportunities for U.S. businesses seeking to establish a place 
in the Russian market. We agree that the situation calls for 
flexibility/that will enable Russian-American commerce to grow 
while Russia's transition to a money-based'free market is 
underway. 
Increasing Russia's reliance on countertrade, however, will 
tend to increase distortions in its allocation of resources and 
allow it to slide back into the pattern of barter that 
characterized trading within the Soviet bloc. Over the long 
term, only a complete transition to a market economy, with the 
most efficient allocation of resources possible, will enable 
trade and investment between our two countries to flourish. 
Our current policy, which is embodied in the trade 
agreement that we have with Russia, opposes government-mandated 
countertrade. Should U.S. companies choose tonengage in 
countertrade, of course, the U.S. can facilitate the 
transaction by providing advisory services and counter-trade 
related information, as well as export credit agency support on 
a case-by-case basis.

January 3, 1994 
Personal 
Danielle and I greatly appreciated your beautiful 
Christmas card with the great photograph of the Gore family. 
We send you our warmest good wishes for your continued success 
and good fortune in 1994. 
From talking to Leon and Tobi Gati this past week and 
from reading the press accounts, it is clear that your visit 
to Russia and other former Soviet Republics was extremely 
important as well as very timely. The Gore-Chernomyrdin 
accords will put in motion the kind of practical measures of 
cooperation that are long overdue. A special word of 
congratulations for Tipper for her good work in St. Petersburg. 
In the fall of 1991 I convinced my friend Andrei Kozyrev 
to invite Paul Volcker to Moscow with a view to making him an 
unpaid financial advisor to Yeltsin and the Russian govern-
ment. Paul and I visited Moscow in February 1992 and met with 
Gaidar, Khasbulatov, Matyukin (then head of the Central Bank) 
and Gerschenko (who succeeded Matyukin in that post a few 
months later). Volcker's appointment never materialized 
because too many people with vested interests felt threatened, 
including Jeffrey Sachs. 
Paul and I came away from Moscow deeply concerned.on two 
major points. First, Gaidar was pushing ahead with decontrol 
of prices unaccompanied by speedy denationalization and 
demonopolization. This was the beginning of the hyper-infla-
tion that pushed at least half of the Russian people below the 
poverty line. Price decontrol before competitive conditions 
had been created simply permitted monopoly state enterprises 
to jack up prices rather than bring more goods to market. 
Our second concern derived from the attitudes expressed 
by Matyukin and Geraschenko. They made it clear that they 
favored an expansive monetary policy and limitless credits to

the large and uncompetitive state enterprises for the purpose 
of maintaining employment. Paul and I felt that the overly 
permissive monetary policy followed by the Central Bank (under 
the effective control of Khasbulatov) would undermine the 
market-oriented adjustment program of the Yeltsin government. 
Looking back, I would say that Gaidar and company applied 
the wrong kind of "shock therapy," and the Central Bank then 
destroyed the Gaidar program by irresponsibly pumping up the 
supply of money and credit. 
It is true that Western aid was too little and too late, 
but if we are looking for the causes of Zhirinovsky's success, 
I believe they lie mainly in the flawed economic policies of 
both the reformers and their opponents -- as well as in the 
political mistakes of the reformers in the election campaign. 
As to where we go from here, I believe you are right in 
urging that we not allow Zhirinovsky's success to diminish the 
western aid effort, but rather that we ameliorate the 
desperate economic and social conditions that now afflict the 
majority of the Russian people. 
As a final note, I sincerely hope that you and Tipper 
will visit Madrid in 1994. It would greatly assist our 
interests here. 
With warm regards to you, Tipper and the family, in which 
Danielle, Nina and Tony join, 
Sincerely, 
Richard N. Gardner

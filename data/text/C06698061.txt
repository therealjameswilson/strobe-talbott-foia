SUBJECT: The Acting Secretary's Meeting With Bosnian Foreign 
Minister Sacirbey: Memorandum of Conversation 
Date: December 26, 1995 
Place: Your Office 
Additional Participants: Ambassador Sven Alcalay; Ambassador 
Gallucci, Ms. Nuland (D Staff), EUR Notetaker 
Summary: Sacirbey made the following points: 
Military stabilization: Bosnia has received offers of 
technical expertise in arms control from several countries, 
and requests U.S. help in coordinating a response such 
offers. Bosnia also has received overtures on providing 
military equipment and training, and seeks U.S. views on 
how it should respond. 
Demobilization: The GOBH is ready to reduce its forces from 
about 200,000 to 40-50,000, but needs advice and material 
assistance in creating programs to support demobilization. 
PFP: Bosnia seeks U.S. U.S. views on its possible future 
membership in PFP. 
Sarajevo gas bill: Russia is pressing for a $4 million dollar 
payment toward arrears on Bosnia's gas bill by Japuary 1, 
and could shut off the gas if it is not paid. The 
Netherlands has offered substantial help. 
Sarajevo Serbs: The G6BH now understands it is in its interest 
for the Serb population to remain. Bosnian Serb authority 
structures must be removed, however. Creative solutions 
are possible to establishing a police force. 
war Crimes: The GOBH wonders if the U.S. is sharing all the 
informatiSfrit-has with the Tribunal. Indictments could be 
handed down on Federation officials. 
End summary. 
Equip and train, military stabilization. In response to 
Sacirbey's question, the Acting Secretary briefly explained 
that Jim Pardew's office would be situated in the State 
Department and would focus on equip and train, though Pardew 
would necessarily have to follow broader issues of military 
stabilization and arms control.

Sacirbey said the GOBH wanted to consult with the U.S. on 
how to train its military and diplomatic personnel on arms, 
control matters, such as the mechanics of verification. He 
said offers of assistance had already come from Canada, the 
Netherlands, Turkey, and elsewhere. He sought U.S views on 
whether this assistance could be accepted instead of or in 
combination with U.S. assistance. He said the Turks had been 
"marketing themselves" to the GOBH aggressively, and inferred 
that Turkey expected to benefit by inclusion in the business of 
reconstruction. 
Sacirbey then said the GOBH was talking about equip and 
train with several countries that are considered friendly by 
both Bosnia and the U.S.: Turkey, Malaysia, Saudi Arabia, 
Egypt (and the Netherlands?). Bosnia did not want to accept 
other offers if this might prevent possible future U.S. 
assistance. Sacirbey asked if Bosnia should pursue this 
subject now with these countries, and if Bosnia should be 
raising this subject with friendly members of Congress. 
The Acting Secretary stressed the importance of keeping in 
close contact on this subject to minimize the risk of 
misunderstanding. He explained that Jim Pardew working on a 
consortium of countries that would be involved in equip and 
train, and noted that opinion on the Hill on equip and train 
was deeply divided. Equip and train was part of a military 
equilibrium formula that is essential both to Bosnia's security 
and peace in the region. 
Ambassador Gallucci noted that we wanted OSCE to-take the 
lead on arms control so the U.S. would not appear to be the 
friend exclusively of one or another party. We would want to 
make our assistance in this area available to all the parties: 
On offers of assistance on arms control matters, Ambassador 
Gallucci said Canada and the Netherlands had valuable 
experience to offer. - - 
Sacirbey proposed that the U.S. play a role in organizing a 
few countries -- including Canada, the Netherlands, and perhaps 
Turkey -- to discuss what could be done to help Bosnia on 
issues of arms control and military stabilization. He noted 
the Dutch and Canadians probably would not be enthusiastic 
about equip and train. Ambassador Gallucci replied that the 
issue was one of technical expertise, and said we would 
consider it and get back with a response. 
Demobilization. Noting that the Federation had two armies, 
Sacirbey complained that the HVO was dominated by the Croatian 
army, and said he hoped a formula for the size of the HVO and 
Bosnian government army could be worked out. On the latter, he 
said current force levels totalling about 200,000 could be 
reduced to 40-50,000, though the army would want to improve its

noted that advice and resources would be needed to support such 
a large demobilization effort, and that programs including 
preferential treatment in loans and housing for veterans would 
be an important element. 
PFP. Noting that he raised the question of Bosnia's PFP 
application with NATO SYG Solana in Brussels, Sacirbey said he 
saw Bosnia's relationship with NATO extending beyond the 
one-year IFOR deployment. He noted that he raised this subject 
in a meeting earlier in the day (12/26) with General Clark at 
the Pentagon. Solana was new and not as ready as Willi Claes 
had been to support Bosnia's application. Sacirbey conceded 
that Bosnia's situation, with three distinct armies operating 
on its territory, was anomalous, but he claimed that Russia at 
least would not object since Bosnia had never belonged to the 
Warsaw Pact. Sacirbey noted that other countries in the 
region, including Slovenia, were already in PFP, and complained 
that when NATO briefings were given only to Croatia or to 
Croatia before Bosnia, it reinforced Tudjman's tendency to 
patronize the Bosnians. 
The Assistant Secretary said that as Bosnia moves towaid 
the future outlined in the Dayton agreement, it is natural that 
it should move toward other goals such as membership in PFP. 
He noted that the Dayton agreement establishes Bosnia as a 
single state and this ultimately will mean a single security 
structure and participation as one unified state in 
international organizations. 
Russian gas bill. The Russians had demanded a-$4 million-
guarantee by January 1, in order to keep the gas on, Sacirbey 
said. Dutch minister for foreign development Pronk had 
provided a large pledge to offset this bill; the Turkish pledge-
was really a proposed reduction of an old Russian debt, which 
Gazprom would probably not accept. The Dutch pledge could 
serve as cofinancing through the. World.bank, Sacirbey asked 
for U.S. help in meeting the gas bill, in averting the 
threatened cut-off in supplies, and in getting the best 
possible financing terms. Ambassador Alcalay-interjected that 
he was following up with the World Bank in Washington, 
Returning to the subject of coordinating offers of 
technical expertise -- this time in administrative and 
financial matters -- Sacirbey suggested that some structure be 
created that would admit countries like the Netherlands and 
Sweden, which were not represented on the PIC Steering Board.

Sarajevo Serbs. Sacirbey complained that after his meeting 
with Bosnian Serb "assembly speaker" Krajisnik in Pale today, 
Admiral Smith had said he was favorably inclined to extending 
the deadline on transfer of Serb-held areas of Sarajevo to the 
Federation, in keeping with the Dayton agreement. The Acting 
Secretary noted that we had seen several wire service reports 
of Admiral Smith's comments, and that they were rather 
contradictory. We were waiting for a readout of the meeting 
from Sarajevo. 
Sacirbey said it was premature to speak of an extension 
when the original 45-day period still had several weeks to go. 
He suggested possible structures for the police in these areas, 
including a "mixed" UN-Bosnian one and an "integrated" 
Muslim-Serb-Croat one. Sacirbey expressed particular concern 
over High Representative Bildt, who he said had assured Bosnian 
Serb officials that the French contingent was friendly to them 
and the Serb authorities would not be removed when the deadline 
fell. 
Digressing on the subject of Bildt, Sacirbey said the High 
Representative was inclined to give in to whatever pressure was 
exerted on him at any given moment, and that Bildt had shown 
evidence of this at the Brussels Conference. Bildt did not 
understand that the Republika Srpska leadership was no longer 
relevant in Sarajevo, though he did understand he could no 
longer deal with Karadzic and Mladic. Sacirbey noted that 
Bildt deputy Michael Steiner was coming to Sarajevo soon, and 
said the GOBH worked well with Steiner. 
- - 
The present Bosnian Serb authorities would have to-be 
removed, although Bosnian Serb individuals should stay. 
Sacirbey acknowledged the GOBH might not have_done_enough 
previously to convince the Serbs they should remain. Now, 
however, government leaders including Izetbegovic realized it 
was in Bosnia's interest that the Serbs stay, both to reinforce. 
Bosnia's commitment to multiethnicity and to support the effort 
of Muslim and Croat refugees to return to their homes in 
Serb-controlled areas. 
Ambassador Gallucci pointed out that we did want the 
Bosnian Serb population to remain in Sarajevo. In that sense, 
there was sense in pointing out that French presence there was 
reassuring to this population. 
War crimes. Sacirbey suggested the U.S. and Bosnia might 
develop a joint working document to treat a variety of issues, 
and said he would be taking the lead on these issues if he 
stayed in the government. He then announced that he was about 
to raise a sensitive issue. He had learned from a meeting with 
Prosecutor Goldstone that the International Tribunal would

indict "one of our people" for war crimes. The Bosnian 
government would cooperate politically, though practically 
there would be .some resistance and a tendency to reduce the 
matter to "us versus them." 
The Acting Secretary said the U.S. position on the Tribunal 
was clear: all parties and all states must cooperate fully with 
it. He emphasized he was not addressing any hypothetical 
situation or commenting on any particular individual. While 
some people claimed the U.S. did not want one or another 
political leader participating in the peace process to be 
indicted, the Acting Secretary said, the truth is that we want 
the Tribunal to carry out its work fully and successfully, 
since there cannot be peace without justice. 
Sacirbey referred to "a feeling" that the U.S. is not 
sharing all the information it has on evidence of war crimes. 
The Bosnian government had collected some relevant intelligence 
and it was hard to believe that the U.S., with far greater 
resources, did not have more. The Acting Secretary reiterated 
U.S. support for the Tribunal and noted the support we have 
extended, limited only by normal restrictions on sharing such 
information.

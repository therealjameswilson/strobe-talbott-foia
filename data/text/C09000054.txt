Dear Strobe: 
I want you to know how much I have appreciated the 
chance to meet with you and your colleagues with regard to 
the opportunity of becoming a Special Adviser to the 
President, coordinating and supervising the bilateral 
programs of this country with regard to Russia and other 
newly independent states of the former Soviet Union. I am 
most grateful to you for the amount of time you have spent 
on this matter, and I particularly appreciated the few 
minutes with Secretary Christopher. 
Let me first state briefly my understanding of the 
facts: 
1. The job as envisaged takes over the Assistance 
Coordination Group which is the focal point of bilateral 
assistance. In total, since 1992 $3.2 billion has been 
appropriated for this purpose of which $1.3 billion has been 
spent or firmly committed, leaving approximately $2 billion 
unspent but firmly planned or budgeted for particular 
purposes. To this amount can be added approximately $850 
million to be appropriated in 1995 with similar declining 
amounts from 1996 onwards. Consequently, it appears that 
there is approximately $2.8 billion available which will be 
expended between now and 1996. 
2. AID is the principal recipient of the funds which it 
either administers itself on AID programs or passes through 
to other programs such as the Peace Corps, USIA and special 
programs designated by members of Congress; for example, 
support of Ukraine, special scientific interchange to keep 
scientists employed, and so on. Particular emphasis has 
been given to enterprise funds for which over $750 million, 
(including Central Asia Fund and Mike Blumenthal's "FLER") 
has been committed over the coming two to three years, as 
well as funds for the completion of the highly successful 
privatization program and planned expenditures for ensuring 
that markets are maintained and administered correctly.

3. For the future it is anticipated that there will be 
"sharp program reductions" after 1996 with the government 
policy being stated as follows: "We believe NIS reformers 
are capable of reorienting their polities and economies by 
the end of the decade. If not, the failure will have been

(I cannot refrain from observing that this seems to me to be

4. Part of this bilateral assistance is provided through 
the Trade and Investment Initiatives utilizing OPIC, Export 
Import Bank, TradeDevelopment Agency, U.S. Department of 
Commerce, MFN Trade Status, Generalized System of 
Preferences, and Bilateral Investment Treaties. 
5. There are also the activities of the Department of 
Defense ("Nunn-Lugar") relating to denuclearization and 
demilitarization which are appropriately administered by the 
Department of Defense, and which I believe would require 
little coordination. 
6. Finally, and most importantly, the United States has 
taken a leading role in the international arrangements which 
cumulatively dwarf the bilateral arrangements and which 
include the activities of the international finance 
institutions and multilateral arrangements such as G-7 
agreements. President Clinton took a leadership role in 
this matter in Tokyo last July.

9. The management of the total process of support for the 
former Soviet Union comes under your supervision at the 
level of the NSC and Thomas Simons, Jr. reports to you as

10. The role of the Vice President seems to be important

11. I have not mentioned the problems in Russia and the NIS 
relating to breakdown in government, in economic structure 
and, most particularly, in the rise of the criminal element. 
I do not need to expand on this subject to you who are 
expert, but let me say simply that there are limits placed 
on what we can do caused by the current situation in these 
countries. (Not to appear t9 be too negative on the 
subject, I also feel that a well organized international 
program could help them solve some of their problems.) 
Having given the broadest possible statement of what I 
believe to be the structure, I should now like to make some 
personal observations: 
a) The bilateral programs are very important but they are 
a small part of the total assistance package. 
b) These programs must be properly administered and have 
been subject to criticism most notably by Congressmen 
Gephardt and Michel in their April memorandum on the 
subject. The draft response of the Administration which I 
have read reflects the belief that in fact the lack of 
progress has been due principally to bureaucratic problems 
and that many of the early difficulties have been solved and 
that the programs are now likely to move forward rather 
quickly. Certainly those leading the individual projects

question is whether or not there is a need to have an 
additional senior person working full time on all the 
programs of this country, to bring the issues to the 
attention of the Congress and the voters and to think 
constructively and entrepreneurially all the time about how 
to win the peace as we have won the Cold War. 
It is obvious that I am only a five-day expert on this 
subject, and for this reason I suggest to you that it might 
be worthwhile to have the President appoint someone to 
review the effectiveness of all our programs and to make

efforts. I had naively thought that such an examination 
would be useful and non-confrontational, which might lead 
either to a reaffirmation of the current structure reporting 
to you or to a different structure reporting to you, to the 
Secretary of State and to the President.
